What happens at a cacao ceremony?

A beginner’s guide to the usual flow of a cacao ceremony, what to wear and bring, and who should check with a doctor first.

Book / view services ↗ Read the article ↓

By Vanessa Yana Kuntur · Published 28 November 2026

Where does ceremonial cacao come from?

Cacao is native to the Americas. In Mesoamerica, the region that includes present-day Mexico and Central America, peoples such as the Maya and the Aztecs prepared cacao as a drink and used it in ritual, feasting and daily life. Today’s cacao ceremonies draw on that history, and often on other Indigenous traditions as well. Practices vary between communities, teachers and facilitators, so no two ceremonies are exactly alike.

Ceremonial-grade cacao is a minimally processed form of the cacao bean, prepared as a drink and shared with intention.

The usual flow

Each facilitator shapes the gathering in their own way, but many ceremonies follow a similar arc.

1. Welcome

Guests arrive, find a comfortable place in the circle and are welcomed. The facilitator usually explains what will happen, and you do not need any previous experience.

2. Intention

There is a moment to set an intention. This can be as simple as a word or a feeling you would like to bring to the time together. You are not usually asked to share it aloud.

3. Drinking the cacao

The cacao is prepared and offered to each person. Many facilitators say a few words of gratitude first. You can drink at your own pace.

4. Music or a sound journey

After the cacao, there is often quiet time with music or a sound journey. Guests usually sit or lie down and simply listen. If you are curious about the sound element, see the sound bath in Sydney page.

5. Sharing

Some ceremonies include a sharing circle, where people can speak about their experience if they wish. Passing is always fine.

6. Close

The facilitator brings the gathering to a close with thanks, and guests often stay for a few minutes of quiet conversation before leaving.

What to wear and bring

In general terms, wear comfortable, loose clothing and consider layers, since rooms and gardens can change in temperature. A water bottle is useful, and some people like to bring a notebook, a blanket or a cushion. Hosts differ on what they provide, so it is best to ask when you enquire. Arriving a little early and unhurried helps you settle in.

Who should check with a doctor first?

Cacao is a food, and it contains naturally occurring stimulants. This is general information, not medical advice. Please speak with your own doctor or health professional before attending if any of these apply to you:

  • You are pregnant or breastfeeding.
  • You take regular medication, including antidepressants or MAOIs.
  • You have a heart condition or have been advised to limit stimulants.
  • You have food allergies or dietary needs.

Please also mention any of these to the facilitator when you enquire. If you are unsure, it is always fine to skip the cacao, and a sound journey can be offered without it.

Taking part with respect

Because cacao has deep roots in Indigenous cultures of the Americas, many people like to approach a ceremony with gratitude for where it comes from. You can do this simply: arrive with an open mind, follow the facilitator’s guidance, and ask about the source of the cacao and the traditions the ceremony draws on. Taking part is always your choice, and you can step out or sit quietly at any time. Every gathering has its own pace, so it is also worth asking how long it will run.
