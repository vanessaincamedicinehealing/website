What is a Q’ero paqo?

A plain-language introduction to the Q’ero people, the role of the paqo and the Andean ideas behind the practice.

Book / view services ↗ Read the article ↓

By Vanessa Yana Kuntur · Published 28 November 2026

Who are the Q’ero?

The Q’ero are an Indigenous Quechua-speaking people of the high Andes in the Cusco region of southern Peru. Their communities sit at high altitude, and life there has traditionally revolved around herding alpacas and llamas and growing potatoes and other highland crops. From the mid-twentieth century onward, outside interest in Q’ero traditions grew considerably.

The Q’ero are a living people, with their own communities, leaders and ways of deciding what is shared and with whom. This article is a general introduction written by a practitioner in Sydney. It is not a statement on behalf of any Q’ero community.

What is a paqo?

Paqo (also spelled paco) is a Quechua word for an Andean ritual specialist. In English it is often translated as “shaman” or “priest”, though neither word fits perfectly. A paqo is usually understood as someone who looks after relationships: between people and the land, between a community and the mountains around it, and between the everyday world and the wider Andean world of spirit.

Common tasks include making offerings to the earth and mountains, leading ceremonies and giving guidance to the community. Some accounts describe different levels of paqo, such as pampa mesayoq and altomesayoq. How those levels are explained, and what they involve, varies between communities and teachers.

Traditionally the path is learned over many years, through apprenticeship with elders and through lived experience, rather than through a short course.

Andean ideas you may hear about

Several ideas come up again and again in Andean teachings. As with most living traditions, practices vary between communities and teachers, so these are short, general descriptions.

  • Ayni is sacred reciprocity: the idea that life is interconnected and that giving and receiving are kept in balance, with respect, gratitude and service.
  • Pachamama is Mother Earth, honoured as a source of life.
  • Apus are sacred mountain spirits, respected as protectors of the places around them.
  • Amaru (serpent), Otorongo (jaguar) and Kuntur (condor) are Andean animals that appear in teachings as symbols. Teachers explain their meanings in different ways.

The Prophecy of the Condor and the Eagle

This is a story told in many modern Indigenous-inspired teachings. It speaks of a time when two paths, long separate, would come together again: the wisdom of the heart and the wisdom of the mind. The Condor stands for intuition, spirit, compassion and connection to the Earth. The Eagle stands for vision, knowledge, science and the achievements of the modern world.

The prophecy is told in several versions, and its origins and wording vary depending on who is telling it. Many people read it as an invitation to bring the two paths into balance rather than as a prediction of specific events.

Learning respectfully

If this interests you, it helps to read widely and to look for Andean and Q’ero voices where you can. Ask any teacher about their training and lineage, be cautious of anyone who claims to speak for all Q’ero people, and remember that a culture is more than a set of practices to be collected. Reciprocity (Ayni) is a good starting point: take care in what you receive, and give something back.
